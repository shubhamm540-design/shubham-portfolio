1 · Creators are the ad engineSix in ten of the live ads I read run through creator partnerships, with a "comment for link" DM hook. It scales reach fast and is hard to control on claims and price.
2 · One hero, many price storiesThe capsule cream is the product. Yet "Buy any 3" shows ₹1,099, ₹1,299 and ₹1,699 on rotating banners, an ad says ₹999 / ₹1,599, and Amazon lists a combo at 76% off.
3 · The first screen costs trustA login pop-up appears on scroll before any value is shown. Real-user layout shift is 0.7 and tap response is 464 ms on mobile.
My take

Suroskie has found a product people share. The next win is not more creators: it is one believable price, a calmer first visit and a reason to come back for the second jar. Everything below is built from public evidence and tagged by how sure I am.

Key Findings & Opportunities

  1. Make one price story: the same offer on banner, ad, Amazon and product page.
  2. Move the login pop-up after value (add-to-cart or checkout) and test it.
  3. Fix layout shift and tap response before adding more paid traffic.
  4. Build a creator scorecard (code, sales, claims) and keep the top 10, not the top 100.
  5. Every revenue and customer number here is a company claim. I used none as an input.

  • IMARC has India skincare at $9.06B in 2025, $10.32B in 2026 and $18.38B by 2034, a 7.49% CAGR (IMARC). Ken Research reads $3.17B in 2025 rising to $5.37B by 2032 (Ken Research).
  • Vyansa says about 75% of skincare sales were offline in 2026 (Vyansa). A D2C brand is competing for the online quarter.
  • A vendor blog puts India D2C beauty and personal care at $4.09B in 2025 growing 36.4% a year to 2032 (ClickPost). That is a vendor estimate; treat it as direction only.
  • exchange4media notes more brands entering Korean skincare, so attention and marketplace visibility get harder, and repeat purchase is the real prize (exchange4media).
Why it worksRoutine skincare is repeat by nature. Korean-style capsules and peel-offs are easy to demo in short video.
Why it is hardHundreds of look-alike capsule creams. Amazon search already shows several below ₹1,000.
Why trust mattersSkin claims are checked by buyers, reviewers and regulators. Price confusion hurts most here.
My take

The market is big enough for a brand this size. The question is not demand but share of the repeat routine, and which product holds the shelf when rivals copy the capsule.

Key Findings & Opportunities

  1. Quote one market definition and state its scope.
  2. Aim at the routine (cleanse, treat, protect), not one hero product.
  3. Treat offline share as a later question; online is the home turf.

Positioning

"Where science meets luxury. Your glow, our mission." Premium words, promo prices and creator voice.

SignalWhat I sawTension
Words"Where science meets luxury", "accessible luxury", "India's first Capsule Cream, powered by Korean tech, made for Indian skin"Premium and scientific
PriceCapsule cream ₹999 single; offers like "Buy any 3 at ₹1,099"; struck prices up to 3× the sale priceDeal-led
VoiceCreators say "obsessed", "game changers", "unexpected results"Casual and personal
ProofSelf-described clean and cruelty-free; no named lab, test or certificate on the About pageWords without a source
  • The brand sells the idea of a smart, Korean-style routine at a mass-premium price. That is a good gap in a market that splits between cheap and clinical.
  • The promise "science" needs a visible source: a test, an ingredient percentage and a named reviewer. One ad names 4% tranexamic acid, which is the kind of detail that builds trust Observed ↗.
  • Hypothesis: a buyer who sees ₹999 on one page and ₹1,699 for two on another reads "discount shop", not "luxury".
My take

The positioning is strong. The price behaviour and the missing proof pull it down. One of the two has to give: the luxury words or the permanent promo.

Key Findings & Opportunities

  1. Put the active ingredient and its percentage on every hero product card.
  2. Publish a short "how we test" note.
  3. Cut the number of live price points per product to one.
